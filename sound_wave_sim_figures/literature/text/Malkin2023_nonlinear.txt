Ultraleap LTD, Glass Wharf, Bristol, England

ARTICLE INFO          ABSTRACT

Non-linear acoustics  application to acoustic levitation and mid-air haptics relies on high amplitude focussed fields
Saturation            capable of generating very high acoustic pressures (e.g. >150 dB SPL). At these elevated pressures
Wave propagation      non-linear propagation effects can become significant, for example: the generation of harmonic
                      frequencies, waveform asymmetry and acoustic saturation. In this work the basics of non-linear
                      acoustics are presented with emphasis on energy loss mechanisms. Experimental work shows
                      non-linearities as a function of focussing distance and driving amplitude. Large 2D microphone
                      scans normal and tangential to the acoustic axis are carried out. Comparisons to linear models are

1. Introduction

    In recent years there has been a large amount of academic and industrial interest in air-borne ultrasonic arrays for a range of
applications, such as acoustic levitation [1,2] mid-air haptics [3,4] amongst others [5]. These applications rely on large amplitude
waveforms with peak pressures often exceeding 150 dB sound pressure level (SPL, re: 20 Pa) where non-linear effects become readily
apparent. In this work a brief introduction to the topic of non-linear acoustics in air is presented along with a discussion on the im
plications to high amplitude array function. Specifically, the losses induced by non-linearity are explored as linear theory will
over-estimate achievable pressures. The phenomena of acoustic saturation is presented where the acoustic field pressure is unrelated to
the source amplitude. Non-linearly induced losses are especially important in acoustic levitation and mid-air haptics as both rely on
acoustic radiation pressures which are proportional to the square of the acoustic pressure. As such, observed forces at elevated
pressures diverge from linear theory considerably.

    Acoustic pressure waves in air can be well-described by a linear wave equation when the dynamic pressure of the wave is much
smallerthan the static pressure of the medium, which in this case is one atmosphere. This adiabatic speed of sound, c0, is given by c0
= P0/0 where  is the ratio of specific heats, P0 is the ambient atmospheric pressure and 0 is the ambient density. However, when
the dynamic pressure approaches a substantial fraction of the ambient pressure, we can no longer assume first-order adiabatic

The level of distortion can be separated into three loosely defined regimes. For  = 0 the waveform is said to be undistorted, for 0 <

A basic explanation of saturation is: (1) An initially sinusoidal waveform of sufficiently high amplitude propagates into a non-linear

medium. (2) As it propagates away from the source it will begin to distort and generate harmonics. (3) The waveform will then become

a well formed saw-tooth rich in harmonics. (4) Due to frequency dependant damping the higher harmonics are quickly damped

limiting the amplitude of the waveform. (5) With the harmonics damped as the waveform propagated further it reduces in amplitude

and reverts back to a sinusoidal form, known as `old age' [9]. Greater source amplitude simply results in more thermal loss at the

pressure discontinuity [11]. This is shown illustratively in Fig. 1c which shows a simulation of severe wave steepening extrapolated to

a non-physical pressure profile. For a rigorous description of the above the reader is directed to reference [8].

This presents an experimental condition where a microphone measurement (assuming infinite bandwidth) is able to record the saw-

tooth waveform but no ability to infer loss. Therefore, the only method by which to verify the presence of significant losses as expected

by acoustic saturation is either by low-amplitude extrapolation or comparison to linear theory.

4. Experimental data

    An Ultraleap 16 × 16 element array was positioned at x = y = z = 0 mm with the acoustic axis being normal to the xy plane. The

array uses muRata MA40S4R transducers operating at 40 kHz with a single transducer outputting 114.3 dB SPL at 300 mm on-axis at
20 Vpsqpuare. For efficient control of the amplitudes and phases each transducer is driven by a pulse width modulated (PWM) signal. While

the PWM signal contains multiple frequency components (dominated by the 40 kHz carrier signal) the acoustic output effectively only

contains the 40 kHz pure-tone due to the sharply tuned resonance of the transducer. The transducers themselves are not free of non-
linearity when driven at elevated voltages. For voltages < 20 Vpsqpuarethe transducers linearly produce around 0.52 Pa/V at 300 mm on-
axis. At 30 Vpsqpuarethis output is reduced to 0.46 Pa/V, a 12% reduction. This amplitude dependant response has been accounted for in

the presented models via a amplitude mapping function. The acoustic signal produced was found to only contain the 40 kHz carrier

signal and as such the observed non-linearities within the acoustic field are known not to have originated at the transducer. In the
results which follow (unless stated otherwise) all transducers were driven at 20 Vpsqpuare.

    A B&K microphone (Type 4138 ­ 1/8th inch) was held 90 to the acoustic axis. To observe non-linearly induced harmonics mi

crophones can be used if their measurement bandwidth is able to capture at least 1 of the harmonics. In the presented work f0 = 40 kHz
and the microphone is calibrated up to 100 kHz, thus it can only capture the first harmonic. An extrapolated calibration chart is

provided with the microphone allowing sensitivity to 150 kHz at 90 incidence to be estimated, although the error introduced is

unknown.

    For all measurements the microphone was positioned using a Kuka robot arm and the scans were performed within an custom
anechoic chamber compliant with ISO 3745. At each position 216 samples were taken at 390 kHz sampling frequency. Analysis of the

data was performed in Matlab 2022.

4.5. E5 - Refracto-vibrometry

    Another method of measuring the harmonic content of an acoustic field is refracto-vibrometry [25­27]. Briefly, the method works
by measuring the variance in refractive index (and thus acoustic pressure) along a laser path through an acoustic field and returning a
path-integral of variance. As refracto-vibrometry is a projection method (similar to schlieren imaging) it is not possible to extract the
spatial variance along the laser path (e.g. a 3D acoustic field is rendered in 2D). The amplitudes of the harmonic frequencies differ from
data captured via microphone (above) and illustrate the inability of this method to correctly capture relative amplitudes. An example
measurement is shown in Fig. 11. The data shows that harmonics as high as the 15th are clearly identifiable strongly suggesting that
the focal point is likely a very well-formed saw-tooth. Similar results have also been shown in a high amplitude acoustic levitator [27].

    It was observed that refracto-vibrometry was unable to detect any wave-form asymmetry (i.e.  = 1) in the time domain. This is
most likely as the LDV system (Polytec PSV-500) is fitted with a DC filter.

5. Discussion

    The physical manifestation of a non-linear speed of sound has profound implications on the operation and function of air-coupled
transducers and arrays. The most significant of these being:

5.1. Focal point amplitude

    Fig. 6 shows that the measured SPL of a focal point reaches a plateau for both `small' and `large' focal distances. For example, the
SPL increase from 20 V to 28 V for F = 50 mm and F = 500 mm are only around 1 dB. Simply put, transducers with similar trans
duction efficiency to muRata MA40S4R capable of operation at any voltage above 28 V would fail to increase the focal point pressure
appreciably. As such if higher focal pressures are desired a good approach would be to: (1) Operate the array at a lower frequency. (2)
Perform the focussing closer to the array (although our results show significant losses even at the relatively close focal distance of F =
50 mm). (3) Use a larger physical array. (4) Where possible, perform the focussing in a medium with a lower non-linearity parameter,
.

5.2. Harmonic generation

The generation of harmonic frequencies at elevated pressures is a classic sign of non-linear behaviour. What is less well studied

however, is the extent to which these harmonics propagate throughout the domain. For example, Fig. 4 shows that when focussing to F

= 200 mm harmonics are generated where the pressure exceeds 120 dB SPL at 150 mm in front of the focal point and extends to more

than 400 mm beyond it. This effect is less pronounced at closer focal distances and more pronounced at greater focal distances, as

expected given the cumulative nature of non-linear propagation.

loss, the hallmarks of saturation.

5.3. Coincident fields

    The harmonic amplitudes can in certain places be higher than the fundamental frequency. For example, in Fig. 4b at z = 450 mm
and Fig. 4c at z = 250 mm the first harmonic, f1, is greater in amplitude than the fundamental, f0. This gives rise to the prominent
`spikes' in the THD in Fig. 5 for F = 50 mm. This behaviour is also observed clearly in the 2D scans shown in Figs. 9d and 10d. The
`spikes' are shown to occur in regions where the fundamental amplitude (Figs. 9a and 10a) is comparatively low. These high amplitude
THDs are most likely due to the spatial distribution of each of the frequencies of interest. In effect the acoustic field can be considered
the summation of each of fundamental and harmonic frequencies. Each of these will have their own spatial distribution with corre
sponding regions of constructive and destructive interference.

linearly with input voltage and energy loss, i.e. more waveform asymmetry means more energy loss. At the relatively far distance of
500 mm however the waveform asymmetry reaches an asymptote where higher driving voltages do not create a more asymmetric
waveform. This can be considered another form of saturation where the asymmetry reaches a maximum which coincides with the
maximum acoustic pressure. The fact that energy loss cannot be observed in the microphone data (either SPL or waveform asymmetry)
means that the energy loss can only be estimated by direct comparison to either linear theory (where possible) or low-amplitude
extrapolation.

5.5. Analytic estimates

    The relatively large errors seen in Fig. 3 suggest that the theory of spherically converging waves in a lossless fluid does not
accurately predict the experimental values. Published data on acoustic saturation in water, [24], shows very good agreement between
theory and experiment. The plateau for F > 200 mm suggests that Eq. (10) may be employed as a guide for saturation pressures in air
for similarly sized arrays where an additional damping of  - 5 dB is included. The error between simulation and experimental data
increases with focal distance. The reason for this discrepancy is most likely due to differences between air and water, specifically the
propagation losses involved. For example, at 40 kHz the damping factor, , is 0.01 and 1.32 dB per metre for water and air respectively.
The lack of a damping factor in Eq. (8) is the result of a loss-less assumption. Szabo states that `In more lossy media other than water,
absorption plays a stronger role in determining levels of saturation' [23]. The saturation in water equations above are useful in un
derstanding underlying principles but quantitative equations for saturation in lossy media (e.g. air) are, to the best of the authors
knowledge, non-existent. We observe that our basic lossy formulation, Eq. (10), gives a small improvement. The improvement is worse
for smaller focal distances most likely due to the breakdown of the assumptions inherent in the gain function, G, as detailed in Section
9.

    Outside of the remarkable behaviour of non-linear acoustic fields there are also considerable experimental difficulties to over
comes. Some of the most significant of which being:

5.6. Microphones

    Microphones are the go-to device for measuring air-borne acoustic fields. At elevated frequencies however (f > 100 kHz) there are
no commercial ones available. This makes the study of harmonic frequencies difficult. In the presented work the evidence strongly
suggests acoustic saturation is occurring and this requires a saw-tooth waveform to occur. The bandwidth of the microphone however
is only capable of measuring the first 2 harmonics, with the sensitivity of the 2nd being beyond the specification. The THDs shown in
Fig. 5 for larger focal distances remain below the theoretical value of 0.60 where other evidence suggests a well developed saw-tooth.
In other words, the second harmonic amplitudes measured with the microphone appear too low, suggesting the calibration factor to be
incorrect. We recall however that this calibration factor is outside of the designated operating range of the microphone.

    If only the fundamental can be measured saturation losses would manifest themselves as a reduction in the relative fundamental
amplitude as the energy is converted into harmonics. There is the additional issue of the microphone itself becoming large enough to
interfere with the acoustic field as its physical dimension approach those of harmonic wavelengths.

5.7. Refracto-vibrometry

    The shortcoming of pressure microphones can be address to an extent using refracto-vibrometry, as shown in Fig. 11, but comes
with the issue of being a qualitative method. It should be noted that non-linear acoustics are weakly non-adiabatic and as such the
speed of light fluctuation cannot be isolated to being a pure pressure measurement as it also detects temperature changes caused by
over-pressure losses [28]. An LDV has the advantage over a microphone of having a very flat, wide frequency bandwidth. We use the
method qualitatively by simply passing the laser beam through the focal point and analysing the time domain data acquired at 2.56
MHz. Attempts were made to scan the acoustic field to `image' the non-linear field around the focal point but the results had a poor
signal to noise ratio (SNR) due to the method being very sensitive to surface conditions of the reflector. The reader is directed to
reference [25] for an introduction to the method along with its limitations.

5.8. Acoustic streaming

    As acoustic waves are damped in the air a momentum exchange between the pressure field and the air molecules takes place [29].
This exchange manifests itself as acoustic streaming, which itself is not restricted to high amplitude acoustics but is more apparent
then. At high acoustic pressures the acoustic streaming can become turbulent where some regions of the field are moving more than
others [30]. Where this fluctuation is small compared to the wavelength the effect may be small, however for higher order harmonics
the wavelength may be sufficiently small for the disturbance to be significant. This may explain why the temporal variance observed in
Fig. 12 is higher for f1 and f2 then f0. This effect may also be in part due to convection currents within the relatively warm test chamber.
As noted before our analysis for energy loss does not take saturation into account as we expect its contribution to be significantly
smaller than due to saturation losses.

5.9. Verification

    The difficulties above make model verification very challenging. Assuming the acoustic field is made up of a high number of
harmonics and the presence of turbulent streaming a computer model capable of capturing these effects would be very complex. Most
non-linear solvers, such as k-wave, are capable of measuring harmonic generation but are not able to capture losses via the shock-wave
`over-pressure' principle shown in Fig. 1c. Therefore, in the absence of suitable simulation tools the challenges of measuring and
predicting non-linear fields will continue.

6. Conclusion

    When using high amplitude air-coupled arrays/transducers the presence of non-linear effects are likely to manifest themselves as
deviations between the often used linear models and experimental measurements. Experimental results show that focal distances close
to the source will have less non-linear effects (and thus losses) compared to greater distances. Our results show that harmonic am
plitudes can be relatively high and in some locations be greater than the fundamental frequency. We also show that saturation
equations modified from water-coupled focussed fields over-estimate maximum achievable pressures. Being aware of the origins and
effects of non-linear propagation can help the design of levitation and mid-air haptics devices (and similar) where losses may be
minimised. This may include the use of lower frequencies as given in Eq. (2). However, the main benefit of ultra-sonic transducers is
their inaudibility. Lower operating frequencies would reduce non-linear losses and increase saturation pressures but their inherently
high SPLs would make the uses limited to acoustic chambers.
