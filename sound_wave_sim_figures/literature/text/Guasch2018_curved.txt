1. Introduction

    The parametric acoustic array (PAA) exploits non-linear propagation of acoustic waves in air to generate audible sound
through piezoelectric ultrasonic transducers (PZTs). In a nutshell, the process goes as follows. An ultrasonic carrier wave
is modulated with an audible signal (primary field) and emitted with a PZT. The air itself demodulates the radiated wave
generating a series of virtual sources of very directive audible sound (secondary field), all along the emitted beam. Since
first proposed by Westervelt [1], the PAA in air has experienced huge progress. This has led to a large variety of potential
applications which comprise from the audio spotlight for personal communication [2,3], to the combination with active
noise control for generating quiet zones [4,5], or to the in situ characterization of the acoustic performance of materials [6,7].
The reader is referred to [8] for a recent review on many PAA developments.

    To date, most PAA applications deal with parametric loudspeakers arrays (PLAs) comprising planar arrangements of PZTs.
Several theoretical models to predict their near and far-field audible pressure have been developed in the past decades. All

such models consider planar devices and radiation into free space. However, some recent developments reflect the need
to extend them to more complex situations. For instance, in [9] a flared horn was connected to a PLA to improve its di-
rectivity and intensity performance, which might be further reinforced in the future through topological optimization [10].
The assumption of free-field radiation is obviously invalid for such a device. Another type of appliances which need tailored
prediction models are those involving PLAs made of PZTs set on curved, rather than planar, surfaces. This is the case in [11],
where a parametric beam focusing source (PBFS) was proposed that involves PZTs set on a cylindrical surface, or the case of
the omnidirectional parametric loudspeaker (OPL) suggested in [12], which consists of hundreds of PZTs set on a sphere to
achieve a continuous collection of audible beams radiating equally in all directions. It is precisely the purpose of this work
to extend the convolution model for the far-field directivity of planar PLAs in [13], to compute the far-field directivity of
curved PLAs.

    In Westervelt's seminal work [1], a prediction formula for the far-field directivity of the PAA secondary field was pro-
posed, where the primary field (carrier+modulator) was assumed to be perfectly collimated. Discrepancies with experiments
led to more precise theoretical developments. Berktay [14] considered amplitude modulation for the carrier and introduced
an aperture factor to improve Westervelt's predictions (see [15] for the inclusion of phase modulation effects in a single
parametric array). More precision was attained at a later time by Berktay himself and Leahy with the inclusion of the
product directivity of the primary waves [16], a model later improved in [17]. Still, this was not enough for the accurate
computation of the far field directivity of PLAs, which typically make use of PZTs with beam widths up to 80°. Recently, a
new approach was proposed which establishes the directivity of the secondary audible field as the convolution of the pri-
mary wave directivities and Westervelt's directivity [13]. This convolution model has shown better prediction capabilities for
PLAs than the preceding ones and, as introduced before, it is the model we intend to complete for arrangements of PZTs set
on curved surfaces. To proceed, we will first need to extend it to three dimensions by considering the emission of a single
PZT on a spherical cone pointing north. Then, by appropriate translations and rotations we will set the PZT at its location on
the curved surface. The procedure has to be repeated for each PZT in the curved PLA and the total secondary field pressure
can be recovered from the summation of all PZT individual contributions, assuming weak non-linearity.

    As an application, we will devise a theoretical prediction model for the far-field emission of OPLs, like the one proposed
in [12]. Omnidirectional sound sources are required for many acoustic tests, such as measuring the transmission loss be-
tween dwellings, or determining the absorption coefficient of material samples in a reverberant test room. Traditionally,
they have been built by placing conventional loudspeakers in the faces of regular polyhedrons; the dodecahedral source
being the most typical case of a regular polyhedron loudspeaker (RPL). Analytical models for the sound field prediction of
conventional spherical loudspeakers can be found e.g., in [18,19]. It is to be noted that for a truly omnidirectional source of
sound, one should perceive no variations in the measured acoustic pressure when moving a microphone at a fixed distance
from the source (e.g., when completing a circular trajectory around it). However, the directivity of a loudspeaker increases
with frequency and standard RPLs lose their omnidirectional character at high frequencies. This manifests as strong dips in
the measured acoustic pressure when placing a microphone in front of one of the RPL vertices. The underlying idea of the
OPL in [12] was to avoid that problem by setting hundreds of PZTs on a sphere that radiate beams in all directions, and
therefore overcome the acoustic pressure dips encountered in standard RPLs.

    A critical point for the proper performance of an OPL is that of finding an appropriate distribution of PZTs on the spheri-
cal surface. This is far from trivial and closely related to the so-called Fekete problem for setting points on a sphere [20­23].
Two different alternatives will be considered in this work. First, a distribution based on the optimum solution of a mechan-
ical force balance problem for points joined by struts on the surface of a sphere [22], and second, an equal-area partitioning
approach [20], which could be more adequate for the practical construction of an OPL. The latter is so for the following rea-
sons. On the one hand the equal-area partitioning distribution enables a proper unfolding of the sphere on a plane, which
could be convenient if the OPL is to be build using flexible printed circuit boards. If, alternatively, a 3D printer is used to
build the casing of the OPL, the equal-area distribution will strongly simplify welding the PZTs connections.

    A preliminary version of some of the contents in this paper was recently presented in [24]. The paper is organized as
follows. Section 2 introduces a brief review of Westervelt's theory for the PAA and overviews the convolution model as well.
That model is then extended for PZTs set in curved surfaces. In Section 3 we focus on the OPL. We first address the problem
of distributing the PZTs on the sphere and then show how the theory in Section 2 can be applied to compute the total
secondary pressure field emitted by the OPL. Numerical simulations comparing the results for the optimal and equal-area
partitioning PZT distributions are presented in Section 4. Conclusions close the paper in Section 5.

2.3. Far-field directivity convolution model for curved parametric loudspeaker arrays

    It is the main purpose of this work to extend the weighted convolution model for a PZT in the preceding section to
predict the secondary acoustic pressure for the difference frequency of PLAs made of a set of PZTs placed on any curved
surface. A sketch of the general problem we want to solve is presented in Fig. 3, where several variables and notation
are introduced to be used in subsequent developments. Devices such as the OPL in [12] or the PBFS in [11] constitute
particular cases of the general problem in Fig. 3. To compute their far-field directivity we have adopted the following steps
and assumptions:

 · It is considered that each PZT emits the primary field in a spherical cone, the PZT being placed at its vertex. This will
    allow the characterization of its emission in three dimensions (see Fig. 1c).

 · We start assuming the PZT is located at the origin of coordinates with its center axis of emission pointing at 0° in the
    positive direction (North Pole).

 · It is supposed that once placed on a point in the curved surface, the center axis of the PZT is pointing in the normal
    direction to the surface at that point. This is the case for the devices in [11,12]. The generalization of the results below
    to PZTs pointing in another direction but the normal one in the surface is straightforward.

 · We rotate the spherical cone pointing to the North Pole to the direction given by the normal of the PZT at its surface
    location.

 · The PZT is translated to the curved surface.
 · The process is repeated for every PZT of the PLA.
 · We assume weak non-linearity for the secondary field once more, and make the summation of all individual PZT pressure

    contributions to get the total one.

3. The omnidirectional parametric loudspeaker

3.1. Distribution of piezoelectric transducers on the sphere

    As an applied example of the theory presented in the previous section we will consider the case of the omnidirectional
parametric loudspeaker (OPL) introduced in [12]. This consists of a spherical surface with hundreds of PZTs placed on it in
728                                  O. Guasch, P. Sánchez-Martín / Applied Mathematical Modelling 60 (2018) 721­738

order to radiate frequency difference beams in all directions, to get an omnidirectional acoustic field. A correct distribution

of the PZTs on the surface turns to be critical to achieve omnidirectionality. However the distribution of PZTs on the sphere

of the device constructed in [12] was not optimal but mostly handcrafted. Therefore, the next problem we need to face

is that of finding a proper distribution of such transducers on the sphere. This is not a straightforward problem [20]. As

opposed to what occurs for a unit circle, where equally spaced points can be simply obtained from the roots of unity, no

analogous procedure can be found for an arbitrary number of points set on the surface of a unit sphere (note that there

are only five convex regular polyhedra, namely the five platonic solids, the largest one being the icosahedron, which could

    In addition to the optimal PZT distribution, a second option to set the PZTs on the sphere has been considered for
practical construction reasons. This consists in trying to partition the sphere into NPZT equal-area parts (see [20]). To proceed
we divide the sphere into a set of spherical segments and for each one we take the tangent to its mid-line. Then we choose
an interval in the tangent having the diameter of a PZT, and fill every spherical segment with as much PZTs as possible,
keeping them apart a minimum safety clearance. In Fig. 5b we show the equal - area partitioning distribution corresponding
to the same sphere and transducers of Fig. 5a. In this case 634 PZTs could be fitted into the sphere. It is to be noted that
all PZT are now arranged in parallels (see Fig. 5d for a zoomed area), which has several practical advantages with respect
to the optimal distribution. If the OPL spherical casing is to be constructed by means of 3D printing, then all pins would
become aligned for a given parallel, which will greatly simplify the welding process and cable circuitry. Besides, if the OPL
was to be built using flexible printed circuit boards (PCB) we should be able to unfold the sphere surface onto a plane for
appropriate cutting and printing of the electrical tracks.

4.1. General settings

    The following values and assumptions have been taken for the numerical simulations in this section. We consider the
OPLs in Figs. 5a and b with a radius of R = 0.125 m filled with PZTs 16 mm in diameter. As exposed before, the OPL with

4.3. Comparison of the performance of optimal and equal-area PZT distributions on OPLs

    Let us next focus on the comparison of the performance of an OPL, again 0.125 m in radius, when using the equal-area
PZT distribution and an optimal one. In Figs. 14a, b and c we have plotted the far-field SPL for three different half orbits at
4 m from the OPL surface (see Fig. 10), and for a difference frequency secondary field of 2 kHz. Fig. 14a shows the results
for an orbit around the equator, Fig. 14b around half a meridian starting at zero longitude, and Fig. 14c for an orbit with an
inclination of 45°. The blue line in the figures correspond to the optimal PZT distribution and the red one to the equal-area
one. The wide Gaussian is used for the primary field directivity.

    As appreciated from Fig. 14, a very omnidirectional secondary field is obtained for all orbits and for both types of PZT
distributions. The SPLs are almost constant in all cases, differences scarcely surpassing 1 dB. The main variations can be
appreciated in the orbit along the meridian (middle subfigure). When crossing the North and South Poles, the equal-area
OPL performs slightly worse than the optimal one, and losses some uniformity. However, the SPL differences are less than
1 dB, which may be considered acceptable in the building of a real device, if one takes into account all the before mentioned
practical advantages an equal-area distribution could offer (see Section 3.1). Also, if one compares with the variations one
gets at high frequencies from regular polyhedron omnidirectional sound sources (see e.g., [9]).

    In Figs. 15­17 we respectively present results corresponding to half orbits around the equator, a meridian and an incli-
nation of 45°, for the equal-area PZT distribution. Each figure contains SPL values against the direction angle at the three
difference frequencies of 1 kHz, 2 kHz and 4 kHz, and for the primary field wide and sharp Gaussians directivities. It can
be observed once more how very omnidirectional pressure fields are generally obtained for all frequencies and for all orbits.
Sharp Gaussian directivities for the primary field result in higher overall SPL values for the secondary field, than when using
wide Gaussians. Yet, the price to be paid is having a less omnidirectional field than that provided by wide Gaussians. The
highest departures from omnidirectionality are seen at the Poles in Fig. 16, as it was the case for the middle subfigure of
Fig. 14. The effect is clearly more noticeable for the sharp Gaussian than for the wide one, reaching values of 4­5 dB.

    To conclude, we show the consequences of diminishing the number of PZTs on the OPL, in Figs. 18a and b. We con-
template again the results for the three previous half orbits and for the two Gaussian primary fields. The OPL in this case
has a total of 171 PZTs. As noticed from the figure, the secondary field SPL is still rather omnidirectional, with variations of
2­3 dB when emitting with the wide primary field, but it totally losses uniformity when using the sharp Gaussian. The SPL
curves become wavy and the deviations at the Poles become unacceptable for practical purposes.

5. Conclusions

    In this work we have suggested an extension of the weighted convolution model for parametric arrays (PAA) to predict
the far-field audible acoustic pressure of parametric loudspeaker arrays (PLA) set on curved surfaces. The convolution model
improves previous PAA prediction approaches by considering the weighted convolution of the primary field directivity with
Westervelt's directivity. It essentially assumes that a piezoelectric transducer (PZT) not only emits a thin on-axis collimated
beam but a bundle of beams on a sustained angle. Then it provides an expression for the resulting secondary field. To
generalize the model for a PZT placed in a generic curved surface and pointing, for instance, in its local normal direction,
we have first built a three-dimensional version of the convolution model, by considering that the PZT emits its primary field
in a spherical cone. The latter is assumed to be at the origin of coordinates pointing North. Then it becomes rotated to make
its central axis coincide with the direction of the local normal at the surface, and finally gets translated there. Doing so for
every PZT in the PLA allows one to predict the secondary acoustic pressure that the device emits at the far-field. This is
done by assuming weak nonlinearity and applying the superposition principle to add the contributions from all individual
PZTs, to finally get the overall sound pressure level.

    As an application case we have addressed the design of an omnidirecticonal parametric loudspeaker (OPL) made of hun-
dreds of PZTs set on a spherical surface. The main purpose of the OPL is to get a truly omnidirectional sound field at high
frequencies, which cannot be achieved by conventional dodecahedron sources. The distribution of PZTs on the spherical sur-
face is critical to achieve a good performance of the OPL. To that goal we have analyzed two different PZT distributions,
namely an optimal one which is based on solving a Fekete-like problem, and an equal-area based one that is more suitable
for construction purposes. The latter is so because in the equal-area distribution all PZT become aligned in parallels. This fa-
cilitates the welding process and cable circuitry. Moreover, the parallel alignment persists when unfolding the sphere onto a
plane, which would be practical if the OPL was to be constructed using flexible printed circuit boards. Numerical simulations
have revealed that the omnidirectionality of the secondary pressure field resulting from the equal-area PZT distribution is
almost as good as that from the optimal one, so the latter could be used in future OPL prototypes with almost no loss in
efficiency. The simulations have also illustrated several characteristics of the OPL such as the audible pressure dependence
with frequency and distance. The importance of placing PZTs on the OPL whose primary field directivites are as wide as
possible, to get more omnidirectional sound pressure levels, has also been highlighted. Sharp directivites result in higher
pressure levels but at the price of loosing omnidirectionality. The performance of OPLs of different radii and the effects of
reducing the number of PZTs on the surface of an OPL have also been analyzed.
738              O. Guasch, P. Sánchez-Martín / Applied Mathematical Modelling 60 (2018) 721­738

    The extended weighted convolution model presented in this work provides a useful prediction tool for the design of
generic curved PLA prototypes. It is worthwhile mentioning that particular designs of OPLs based on it are currently under
development. Further ongoing work is focused on developing theoretical models for the near-field prediction of the OPL
secondary acoustic pressure field.
